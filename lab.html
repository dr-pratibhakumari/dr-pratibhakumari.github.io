Summary Artificial intelligence is increasingly being adopted in healthcare and agriculture, two nationally important sectors undergoing rapid digital transformation in India. However, most AI models are trained once using a fixed dataset, whereas the conditions under which they operate continue to change. Medical images vary across hospitals, scanners, staining procedures, organs and patient populations. Agricultural images change with crop variety, plant growth, season, weather, illumination, camera configuration and emerging diseases. These distribution shifts can substantially reduce the reliability of conventionally trained models. This proposal aims to develop continual-learning methods that learn from sequentially evolving data while retaining previously acquired knowledge. The research will directly address important limitations of current continual-learning systems, including their dependence on labelled data, assumption of clearly separated domains, difficulty in handling heterogeneous modalities and reliance on large historical-data buffers. Replay-based, regularisation-based, architectural and parameter-efficient strategies will be investigated rather than assuming a single method to be suitable for all settings. The first research thrust will focus on computational pathology, building directly upon the Principal Investigator’s previous work on privacy-aware domain-incremental learning, latent replay, whole-slide image analysis and modality-agnostic medical image segmentation. The proposed research will investigate continual learning under centre, stain, scanner, organ and modality shifts. Particular emphasis will be placed on learning from limited or unavailable annotations, handling non-rigid domain boundaries, incorporating image and textual information where available, and retaining previous knowledge without unrestricted storage of historical patient data. The second thrust will investigate continual learning for smart agriculture, where environmental and biological changes naturally produce long-duration non-stationary data streams. Camera-based crop observations will be collected locally, while public agricultural datasets will enable immediate algorithm development. The work will study crop-health and growth monitoring under changes in seasons, illumination, growth stages, crop varieties and acquisition conditions. Replay-based and non-replay methods will be compared under constraints on storage, computation, bandwidth and annotation. The project will produce new continual-learning methods, a computational-pathology demonstrator, a locally collected longitudinal agricultural dataset, reusable software and benchmark protocols. It will also establish the methodological and collaborative foundation for larger national projects connecting artificial intelligence with digital healthcare and smart agriculture. Revised Introduction 8. Introduction India is rapidly expanding the use of digital technologies and artificial intelligence in healthcare and agriculture. Digital pathology, electronic health records, crop surveillance, sensor-based farming and camera-assisted disease identification are creating increasingly large and diverse datasets. Such data provide major opportunities for decision support, disease monitoring and resource-efficient services. However, their effective use requires models that remain reliable as the underlying data and operating environments change. Most current machine-learning systems are developed under a static learning assumption. A model is trained using a fixed dataset and is subsequently deployed without continuously incorporating new knowledge. This assumption is often unrealistic. In computational pathology, tissue appearance may vary because of differences in staining protocols, scanners, hospitals, tissue preparation, organs and patient populations. In agriculture, data distributions vary across crop cycles, growth stages, weather, illumination, geographical regions, crop varieties, camera systems and disease conditions. Consequently, a model trained under one set of conditions may not perform equally well under future conditions. Repeatedly retraining a model from scratch is expensive and requires access to all previously collected data. It also requires repeated annotation, which is particularly costly for medical images and agricultural disease data. In healthcare, centralising historical data may additionally be restricted by privacy, institutional ownership and regulatory considerations. In agriculture, continuous camera monitoring may generate large volumes of redundant data that cannot all be retained or transmitted by storage- and bandwidth-constrained systems. Continual learning addresses this problem by enabling a model to acquire knowledge from sequentially arriving data while preserving useful information learned previously. The central challenge is to avoid catastrophic forgetting, in which adapting to a new dataset degrades performance on earlier datasets. Existing approaches address forgetting through replay of selected historical samples, regularisation of important model parameters, knowledge distillation, architectural expansion, parameter isolation and combinations of these strategies. Continual learning in medical image analysis is now an active research area, and the Principal Investigator has already contributed to privacy-aware computational pathology, generative latent replay, white-blood-cell classification and modality-agnostic brain-lesion segmentation. These studies provide the preliminary foundation required to move beyond elementary continual-learning settings. The proposed medical research will therefore directly investigate harder practical conditions, including limited or absent labels, multimodal image–text information, missing modalities, non-rigid domain boundaries and restrictions on historical patient-data storage. In comparison, continual learning remains less systematically studied in smart agriculture, even though agricultural environments provide a natural example of continuously changing data. Existing crop-disease models are commonly developed using curated datasets collected under controlled conditions. Their performance may deteriorate under local field conditions, new crop varieties, later growth stages, seasonal changes or different cameras. The proposed project will address this gap through algorithm development on public datasets combined with longitudinal camera-based data collection at IISER Bhopal. Data collection will therefore not precede the research; it will proceed in parallel with method development. The agricultural research will not be restricted to replay-based methods. Replay is relevant because continuous camera streams generate large and repetitive datasets, but regularisation-based, architectural and parameter-efficient alternatives may offer better storage–performance trade-offs. The project will systematically compare these strategies under constrained storage, computation, communication and annotation budgets. Privacy will be considered where camera data capture people, locations or externally owned farms, but it will not be presented as the principal agricultural motivation. The proposed research is organised around two complementary application areas. Computational pathology provides a mature, high-stakes and privacy-sensitive setting supported by the Principal Investigator’s prior experience. Smart agriculture provides an India-relevant setting in which locally acquired longitudinal data and practical field constraints can be studied. Both areas share the same scientific problem: developing AI models that continue to learn under evolving data distributions rather than becoming obsolete after their initial training.
